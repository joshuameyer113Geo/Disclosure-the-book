# Chapter 9 (cross-reference draft): Old Stories, New Witnesses

Status: draft. Footnotes cite permanent Historian IDs (AO- from history/ARYAN_OCCULT.md, MX- from history/MYTH_CROSSREF.md). Footnotes marked **[unverified, no ID yet]** use live web sources that Historian has not yet assigned an ID (see the list at the end).

## Introduction

This chapter puts two bodies of material side by side. One is old: myths, chronicles, and folk accounts of beings who arrive from the sky or the sea, teach or threaten, and leave. The other is recent: reports from pilots, police officers, and ordinary people who say they saw something they could not identify. Some of them say they met someone.

I am not trying to prove that the old stories describe the same thing the modern witnesses report. I want to know what actually repeats and what does not, and where each comparison came from. Most "ancient UFO" parallels were not found in the old texts. Modern writers brought them there, usually after 1947 and usually for readers already primed by flying-saucer news. So each section below asks two questions: what does the primary source say, and who first drew the UFO parallel, and when? Sometimes the second answer is unknown. When it is, I say so rather than guess.

The chapter closes with two harder topics. The first is the cases that fit no pattern. The second is the explanation that skeptics and witnesses both treat with care: that all of this is mental illness.

## The interpreters came first

The habit of reading old records as visitations has a traceable start. In 1919, Charles Fort's *The Book of the Damned* proposed that visitors from the sky could be found in neglected records. W. Raymond Drake, a British follower of Fort, then published *Gods or Spacemen?* in 1964, four years before Erich von Däniken's *Chariots of the Gods?* (1968).[^T-014] Drake went on to issue regional volumes, among them *Gods and Spacemen in the Ancient East* (1968), *Gods and Spacemen in the Ancient West* (1974), and *Gods and Spacemen in Greece and Rome* (1976).[^drake] Those books stand behind most of the parallels below.

## Norse lore: Thor and Odin

**Primary sources.** Thor, the hammer Mjöllnir, and Odin, who wanders and hung on the world-tree for knowledge, come down to us mainly through two Icelandic works. One is the Poetic Edda, preserved in the thirteenth-century Codex Regius. The other is the Prose Edda that Snorri Sturluson compiled around 1220.[^MX-001] Snorri was a Christian writer, and he rationalized the gods as human kings from Troy whom later people worshipped. Colavito points out that the Greeks and the Norse both "rationalized their gods as allegedly real ancient human kings."[^colavito-china] That medieval move is the earliest ancestor of the modern ancient-astronaut argument: the gods were really people, only more advanced.

**Who drew the UFO parallel.** Modern ancient-astronaut media present Thor's hammer and Odin's travels as remembered technology. That much is a pseudoscientific reinterpretation, not a scholarly reading.[^MX-002] The Historian found no author who explicitly connects the Norse gods to the modern "Nordic" alien type.[^MX-004] A shared word is not a shared lineage, and this chapter does not treat it as one. **Open question:** who first connected Thor or Odin to UFOs in print, and when?

## Greek gods

**Primary sources.** Hesiod's *Theogony* (c. 700 BCE) and the Homeric poems describe the Olympians: gods who quarrel, take human lovers, father half-divine children, and come down to intervene in human affairs. Prometheus brings fire to humanity and is punished for it.

**Who drew the UFO parallel.** Drake devoted a whole volume, *Gods and Spacemen in Greece and Rome* (1976), to the classical world.[^drake] That confirms the parallel was in print by the mid-1970s. It does not show that he was the first, and the Historian found no source for a named alien reading of Prometheus or of the Olympians in particular.[^MX-007] **Open question:** the earliest specific alien reading of Prometheus or the Olympians.

## Chinese dragons

**Primary sources.** Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*, 109–91 BCE) describes the culture hero Fu Xi as having "a serpent's body, a man's head." It says that "there being a dragon omen, he enrolled dragons among his officers," and that he drew the eight trigrams.[^colavito-china] In Chinese tradition the dragon is a creature of water, weather, and imperial authority. It is not a vehicle.[^MX-008]

**Who drew the UFO parallel.** Colavito traces a 2012 *Ancient Aliens* claim, that ancient Chinese kings received texts from beings "down from the sky," to the Fu Xi material. He finds no such sky-visitor story in the early sources. In those texts the early rulers act on earth, with only symbolic references to Heaven.[^colavito-china] The best-known "Chinese alien" artifact, the Dropa stones, is a hoax of the 1960s with no verifiable excavation or museum record.[^dropa] **Open question:** the first printed reading of Chinese dragons as craft. Drake's 1968 volume on the "Ancient East" is a likely candidate, but nobody has checked it page by page. The earliest dated example Historian has logged is Hartwig Hausdorf, *The Chinese Roswell* (1998).[^MX-009]

## Shore arrivals: the Utsuro-bune and Xu Fu

**Utsuro-bune (1803).** Several Edo-period texts record that a round vessel drifted ashore in Hitachi Province (now Ibaraki Prefecture) in 1803. Inside was a young woman in unfamiliar clothes who could not speak Japanese and would not let anyone near the box she carried. The fishermen pushed her back out to sea. The texts include Komai Norimura's *Ōshuku zakki* (c. 1815), *Toen shōsetsu* (1825, compiled by the literary circle of Kyokutei Bakin), *Hyōryū kishū* (1835), and *Ume no chiri* (1844).[^MX-010] Kazuo Tanaka, a professor emeritus at Gifu University, has found eleven related documents. He reads the episode as folklore and literary embellishment. In none of the accounts does the vessel fly or move under its own power.[^utsuro-nippon] An exhibition catalogue from the city of Kuki notes that the Edo period had no concept of an unidentified flying object. The vessel was treated as a foreign ship. Only after the postwar saucer reports, from the Shōwa 30s (1955–64) onward, did Japanese magazines begin calling it an Edo-period UFO encounter.[^utsuro-kuki] **Open question:** the Historian found no source naming who first read the Utsuro-bune as a UFO. The decade can be dated. The author cannot.

**Xu Fu (219 BCE).** The *Shiji* records that Xu Fu, an alchemist from Qi, asked the First Emperor of Qin for permission to sail in search of Penglai and the other islands of the immortals. The emperor sent him with several thousand boys and girls.[^MX-011] The *Shiji* does not name Japan. The identification of his landfall with Japan appears in later works such as the *Yichu liutie* of the monk Yichu (951–960).[^MX-011] This story runs the other way from the Utsuro-bune: a mainland expedition becomes, in later tradition, the founding of a people across the sea. **Open question:** no source was found for anyone reading Xu Fu as a UFO or alien episode. He belongs in this chapter as a comparison case for arrival-from-the-sea stories, not as part of the UFO record.

## The Nazi-era search for "Aryan" origins

This material belongs in the chapter because later UFO lore borrowed from it heavily. The historical record and the legend need to be kept apart.

**Thule Society.** It was founded in Munich in August 1918 by Rudolf von Sebottendorff (born Rudolf Glauer) as a cover name for the Bavarian lodge of the Germanenorden, a völkisch, antisemitic order founded in 1912.[^AO-002] Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke's *The Occult Roots of Nazism* remains the standard scholarly account.[^AO-003]

**Ahnenerbe.** The SS Ahnenerbe was founded on July 1, 1935, at the urging of Heinrich Himmler and Herman Wirth. According to Germany's Federal Archives, its purpose was to supply "scientific" proof of "Aryan" superiority. It also housed a research post defending Hanns Hörbiger's World Ice Theory.[^AO-004][^AO-005] Its 1938–39 Tibet expedition was led by the zoologist Ernst Schäfer. The anthropologist Bruno Beger measured Tibetans' skulls and faces to study the "Nordic race in this region."[^AO-006] Isrun Engelhardt's research shows that Himmler pushed for esoteric aims while Schäfer planned a scientific survey and refused to take the World Ice theorist Edmund Kiss along.[^AO-006] The Ahnenerbe was later involved in human experiments and the assembly of a skeleton collection.[^AO-004] That history should not be romanticized.

**Vril.** "Vril" comes from fiction: Edward Bulwer-Lytton's novel *The Coming Race* (1871).[^AO-001] In 1947 Willy Ley described a small Berlin group that searched for Vril, and he wrote that "they knew that the book was fiction."[^AO-008] The "Vril Society" as a powerful Nazi lodge was first claimed by Louis Pauwels and Jacques Bergier in *Le Matin des magiciens* (1960).[^AO-013] In 1992, Norbert Jürgen-Ratthofer and Ralf Ettl merged it with the postwar myth of Nazi flying discs.[^AO-014]

**Schwabenland.** The German Antarctic expedition of 1938–39, aboard the ship *Schwabenland* and led by Alfred Ritscher, surveyed the region it called Neuschwabenland, in present-day Dronning Maud Land. It did not build a base.[^AO-007] In 1974 Ernst Zündel, writing as Christof Friedrich, published *UFOs: Nazi Secret Weapon?*, which claimed that saucers flew from a secret Antarctic base.[^AO-015] Colin Summerhayes and Peter Beeching examined the claim and found no evidence of any wartime German base.[^AO-007]

**Who drew the UFO parallel.** The documented line runs from Bulwer-Lytton's fiction (1871) to Ley (1947), then to Pauwels and Bergier (1960), Zündel (1974), and Jürgen-Ratthofer and Ettl (1992). The parallel was drawn after the war by writers, and some of them, Zündel among them, were openly sympathetic to Nazism.

## Dietrich Eckart as legend

Dietrich Eckart was a poet and the publisher of the antisemitic weekly *Auf gut Deutsch*, which received support from the Thule circle. He was a mentor to the young Hitler, who dedicated the second volume of *Mein Kampf* to him. Eckart died in Berchtesgaden on December 26, 1923.[^AO-009][^AO-010][^AO-011] The legend goes further. A supposed deathbed line, "Follow Hitler! He will dance, but it is I who have called the tune," has no confirmed printing earlier than J. H. Brennan's *The Occult Reich* (1974), a book without notes or bibliography. Trevor Ravenscroft's *The Spear of Destiny* is sometimes named as the source, but Historian could not confirm that it contains the quote, and its publication date is also unconfirmed. Goodrick-Clarke dismissed this whole genre of "Nazi satanism" books.[^AO-012] The quotation has no documented primary source and should not be repeated as fact.

## Nordics and reptilians: modern lore and its roots

**Nordics.** On Nov 20, 1952, near Desert Center, California, George Adamski said he met Orthon, a human-looking Venusian with long fair hair who warned about nuclear testing.[^T-041] Writers have noted Orthon's resemblance to Klaatu in *The Day the Earth Stood Still* (1951). From 1975, Billy Meier in Switzerland claimed contact with "Pleiadians."[^T-052] The label "Nordic" itself is not datable. The Historian found only that it was common by the 1980s.[^AO-020] **Open question:** the earliest use of "Nordic" for an alien type.

**Reptilians.** David Icke went on BBC *Wogan* on April 29, 1991,[^T-053] and published *The Biggest Secret* in 1999. That book claims shape-shifting reptilian bloodlines rule the world. Critics have shown that its structure reproduces antisemitic conspiracy motifs.[^T-043] In a 2013 poll, 4% of US voters said they believed reptilian people control the world.[^T-044] Ancient serpent figures, from the Eden serpent to the Nāgas, were later read as reptilians. That reading is modern, as Chapter 5 shows. The lineage of reptilian lore before Icke, including pulp fiction, the TV series *V* (1983), and Credo Mutwa, remains an open research item (history/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md).

## Outliers

Some reports fit none of the recurring types: not grays, not Nordics, not reptilians. They also did not repeat.

**Cat-faced occupants, Itajubá, Brazil (June 7, 1967).** An ambulance driver, named as Geraldo Baqueiro (also spelled Barqueira), reportedly said a luminous, transparent object stalled his vehicle and that he could see occupants with cat-like faces inside. The case reached Jacques Vallée's landing catalogue. In 1971 O. H. Turner of Australia's Joint Intelligence Organisation copied it, as Case 848, into a secret paper. That paper is now open at the National Archives of Australia (series A13693, item 30030606).[^cat] The Australian file is authentic. The encounter is not verified. It is one witness, and the account comes secondhand through civilian UFO literature. The word "cat-like" records his impression, not an anatomy.

**Quarouble, France (Sept 10, 1954).** Marius Dewilde reported two short, bulky figures in what looked like diving suits near an object on railway tracks, and a light that left him unable to move. The same Australian file lists it as Case 144.[^cat] Small humanoids later became common, so this case is better described as an early example of a type than as an outlier.

**Research need.** I found no documented source for other non-recurring forms, such as insectoid, robotic, or plant-like beings, that would meet this book's standard. The category stays open for Historian.

## The mental-illness explanation

One easy explanation for all of this is that the witnesses are ill. The record does not support that explanation in its simple form. It also does not leave the reports unexplained.

**The argument against illness alone.** Visitor stories appear in many cultures and centuries, and modern witnesses include trained observers. Illness alone has trouble accounting for so much agreement. Controlled research has also not found the expected pathology. Nicholas Spanos and colleagues (1993, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*) compared people who reported UFO experiences with community and student control groups. People with "intense" experiences (contact with beings or missing time) did not score as more psychopathological, less intelligent, or more fantasy-prone than the controls.[^spanos] Susan Clancy and colleagues at Harvard noted in 2002 that "abductees" seldom show signs of mental illness.[^clancy2002]

**The documented counter-explanations.** These same studies point to mechanisms other than illness.

- *Sleep paralysis.* In the Spanos study, intense experiences were more often sleep-related, and intensity correlated with measures of fantasy-proneness and unusual sensory experience.[^spanos] The folklorist David Hufford's *The Terror That Comes in the Night* (1982) documented Newfoundland's "Old Hag." In that waking paralysis the sleeper feels pressure on the chest and senses a hostile presence. Hufford argued that the core experience is shared across cultures and that each culture names it differently.[^hufford] Clancy and Richard McNally (2005) found that the accounts of the alleged abductees they studied matched this pattern.[^clancy2005] This explanation competes directly with the cross-cultural argument above. A shared feature of human sleep could produce shared descriptions without any shared outside cause.
- *Memory and suggestion.* People who reported recovered memories of abduction were more prone than controls to false recall and recognition in the lab. Hypnotic suggestibility predicted those errors.[^clancy2002] Clancy's book *Abducted* (Harvard University Press) describes how a nightmare, a cultural script, and hypnotic regression can combine into a sincere false memory.[^clancy-book]
- *Cultural transmission through media.* The best-known test case is the Hills. Their 1961 accounts describe uniformed, broadly human figures. The large wraparound eyes first appear in Barney's hypnosis session of Feb 22, 1964. Martin Kottmeyer argued in 1994 that they echo *The Outer Limits* episode "The Bellero Shield," which aired twelve days earlier. Jason Colavito notes that the resemblance is partial. The link is **disputed**.[^MC-029] Through Fuller's book and the 1975 TV film, the Hills' account then became the template for later abduction narratives.[^T-012]

**Where that leaves the question.** The studies cited here do not find that experiencers are, as a group, mentally ill. They find ordinary mechanisms instead: sleep, memory, suggestion, and shared stories. None of these studies addresses multi-witness or sensor-recorded military cases, which Part II covers. I have not tried to close that gap here.
