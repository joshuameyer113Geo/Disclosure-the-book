# Chapter 1: Rock Art and the 'Spacemen' Reading

*What the Stones Show, and Who Said They Were Astronauts*

Status: draft. Footnotes cite permanent Historian IDs from history/TIMELINE.md (T-077 to T-080); the underlying sources are S-0357 to S-0376 in history/SOURCES.md. Passages marked **(author analysis)** are interpretation and carry no citation. Open questions are listed in history/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md, items 30 to 33.

## Introduction

Start with a simple problem. Suppose you saw something you had no word for. How would you describe it? How would you draw it?

You would reach for the nearest thing you knew. If a disc were floating on the water, you might call it a pot, because a pot is the closest shape you have a name for. There is no other way to write it down if you do not have the words. On this view, old accounts may record real events in the only language their makers had, and the people who made them may not have understood what they were looking at. Or the old accounts may mean exactly what their makers and their descendants say they mean. **(author analysis)**

That is the question this book carries into the deep past, and this chapter is where it starts. It is a question, not an argument. I am not trying to prove that any figure painted or carved on rock shows a spacecraft or a space traveller. I am also not trying to prove that none does.

What I can do is put three things side by side for each site. The first is what the stones themselves show, as archaeologists have dated and described them. The second is what the people who hold the tradition say, where such people exist. The third is who first called the figures "spacemen" or "astronauts" in print, and when. Almost every one of those readings appeared after 1947, when flying-saucer news gave the public a new picture to compare things to. So each section asks the same two questions used in Chapter 9: what does the primary, archaeological, and custodial record say, and who first drew the spacemen parallel, and when? Where the second answer is unknown, I say so rather than guess.

Four sites follow: Val Camonica in Italy, Tassili n'Ajjer in Algeria, the Wandjina of the Kimberley in Western Australia, and the Great Gallery of Horseshoe Canyon in Utah.

## Val Camonica, Italy: the duellists at Zurla

**What the record says.** Valcamonica, a valley in Lombardy in northern Italy, holds one of the largest bodies of rock engraving in the world. UNESCO lists more than 140,000 figures carved over roughly 8,000 years. In 1979 it became Italy's first World Heritage Site.[^T-077]

The figures that ancient-astronaut writers reproduce come from two rocks: Zurla Rock 1 and Foppe di Nadro Rock 24. The best-known image is a pair of figures at Zurla with rays or projections around their heads. They are Iron Age. An article from CICAP, the Italian skeptics' organization, supervised by Paolo Medici, an excavation director at the Centro Camuno di Studi Preistorici, reads the pair as armed duellists of the seventh century BCE. In that reading the "rays" are crested helmets. What look like round objects in their hands are small round shields seen in profile, and they carry antenna swords of the Tarquinia type. The scene shows an initiation or a ritual duel.[^T-077]

**The custodians.** Here the book's usual check runs out. There is no living descendant tradition at Val Camonica. The Camunni, the people of the valley who made many of the carvings, left no testimony about what the figures meant.[^T-077] Nobody alive can say "these are ours, and this is what they mean." In the usual sense, no custodian objection is possible.

**Who first drew the spacemen parallel.** This is **unconfirmed**. The sources say only that ancient-astronaut writers have used the Zurla figures "since the 1960s." The CICAP article connects the reading loosely to the Italian writer Peter Kolosimo and to Erich von Däniken, but it names no first book and no first year.[^T-077] Kolosimo is a reasonable place to look. Nobody has yet checked his books page by page, so this chapter does not name him or anyone else as the originator. **Open question (OPEN_QUESTIONS #30):** who first printed the "astronaut" reading of the Zurla and Foppe di Nadro figures, in what work, and in what year?

**How the image travels.** The Zurla pair has become one of the most reproduced "ancient astronaut" images in the world. It is often captioned "10,000 BC," a date the archaeology does not support for these figures.[^T-077] The valley's other famous carving, the Camunian rose, is the emblem of the Lombardy region.[^T-077]

The Historian's file adds a note that belongs to interpretation, not fact. With no living custodians, the images are an open canvas. Cropping the two figures out of their duel scenes is what makes the "helmet" reading work, because the context of weapons and combat disappears. **(author analysis)**

Back to the opening question. If a seventh-century engraver wanted to show a warrior's crest, rays around the head are one way to draw it. If a twentieth-century reader has seen pictures of space helmets, rays around a head can look like an antenna. Both readers are comparing the figure to the closest thing they know. Only one of them shared the engraver's world. **(author analysis)**

## Tassili n'Ajjer, Algeria: the Round Heads and Lhote's "Martians"

**What the record says.** Tassili n'Ajjer is a sandstone plateau in the Algerian Sahara and UNESCO World Heritage Site no. 179.[^T-078] Its painted shelters include a style that researchers call the Round Head period, after figures with large, rounded, mostly featureless heads. The British Museum's African Rock Art project gives the Round Head period as about 10,000 to 8,000 years ago. It also notes that the dating is contested: the researchers Henri Lhote, Fabrizio Mori, and Alfred Muzzolini disagree. One Round Head painting in the Acacus, across the border in Libya, has a radiocarbon date of 8590 ± 390 BP.[^T-078]

The mainstream archaeological reading is that the figures show people in ritual masks and costume.[^T-078] One commonly repeated detail needs a correction. "Jabbaren," the place where the most famous figures are, is the Tuareg name for the massif, meaning "giants." It is not a name for any one painted figure.[^T-078]

**The custodians.** The Historian found no published statement from Tuareg or Algerian custodians giving their own reading of the Round Heads or responding to the "Martian" reading.[^T-078] That is a gap in what we have found, not evidence that no such view exists. **Open question (OPEN_QUESTIONS #32):** any Tuareg or Algerian custodian statement on the "Martian" reading.

**Who first drew the spacemen parallel.** Here the record is **confirmed**, and the first person to say it was the archaeologist who brought the paintings to a wide public. Henri Lhote, a French explorer, led an expedition to copy the Tassili paintings and published *À la découverte des fresques du Tassili* in 1958. The English edition, *The Search for the Tassili Frescoes*, followed in 1959.[^T-078]

In the English text, Lhote says that certain round-headed figures are "very like the pictures we sometimes see of 'Martians'." He remarks that this "would make a good heading for a sensational newspaper article." He also uses "'Martian' type" and "'Martian' phase" as working labels for a style.[^T-078]

One phrase needs care. Secondary sources often say that Lhote called one large figure "the great Martian god." That exact phrase was not found in the scanned text of the English edition. It is attributed to him by later writers.[^T-078] This chapter therefore does not quote it as his words. **Open question (OPEN_QUESTIONS #32):** locate the "great Martian god" caption, if it exists, in the 1958 French edition.

**Did he mean it?** This is **disputed**. Lhote's own wording, with "Martians" in quotation marks and the aside about a sensational headline, reads as ironic: a field nickname rather than a theory.[^T-078] Von Däniken and others later borrowed the image, according to secondary sources, and the irony did not travel with it.[^T-078]

**How the image travels.** The popular press seized on "Martians in the Sahara," and the figure became a staple of ancient-astronaut books and television.[^T-078] The Historian's file notes two complications in the record itself. The expedition wetted the paintings, and Lhote later admitted to copies, in the Historian's wording. Both complicate what later readers think they are looking at. (The file places this in its interpretive note; see the Historian list at the end.)[^T-078]

Here the opening question turns around. At Tassili it was not an ancient painter who reached for the nearest familiar word. It was a modern explorer in 1958 who looked at an unfamiliar figure and said, half joking, that it looked like a Martian, because Martians were the nearest picture his readers would recognize. A comparison made for effect became, for later readers, a claim. **(author analysis)**

## The Wandjina, Kimberley, Western Australia

**What the record says.** In the Kimberley region of Western Australia, rock shelters hold paintings of the Wandjina: large figures with round, mouthless faces and haloes or headdresses around their heads. An ANU Press study describes the Wandjina as "the supreme spirit ancestors of the Indigenous people of the Kimberley," tied to the monsoon and rain.[^T-079]

One point matters for any reading of the images. Wandjina paintings are lawfully repainted by those with the right to do so, so their styles are not fixed in one ancient moment.[^T-079] The first European record of them is George Grey's, from his 1838–39 expedition. Grey doubted that Aboriginal people had made the paintings.[^T-079]

**The custodians.** Unlike Val Camonica, the Wandjina have living custodians. The Mowanjum Art and Culture Centre speaks for the Worrorra, Ngarinyin, and Wunambal peoples. For them the Wandjina are creator beings and rain spirits.[^T-079]

**Who first drew the spacemen parallel.** According to the sources found so far, this is **confirmed**. ABC News reports that the alien theory "emerged in" Erich von Däniken's *Chariots of the Gods?* (German edition 1968). The ANU chapter cites the 1971 Corgi English edition and von Däniken's *Return to the Stars* (1972).[^T-079] That makes von Däniken in 1968 the first printing found so far. It does not rule out an earlier one. **Open question (OPEN_QUESTIONS #33):** did anyone print a spaceman reading of the Wandjina before 1968?

**The custodians answer.** Here the record includes something the other sites lack: a direct response from the people whose tradition was being reinterpreted. In December 2016, Leah Umbagai, a former manager of the Mowanjum Art Centre, told ABC News: "It's like people are making fun, or think we're making things up, and it's hurtful for us."[^T-079] ABC described the alien theories as "deeply hurtful" to the Worrorra, Ngarinyin, and Wunambal peoples.[^T-079]

A related case shows how the custodians treat their imagery generally. In 2010, Mowanjum custodians objected to a Wandjina sculpture made by a non-Indigenous artist in Katoomba, New South Wales, and the local council ordered it removed in October 2010.[^T-079] That dispute was about unauthorized use of the image, not about aliens, and it is included here only to show that the custodians actively assert their rights over it.

**How the image travels.** Wandjina appeared in the opening ceremony of the Sydney 2000 Olympic Games.[^T-079] Ancient-astronaut media still describe the headdress as a "helmet." Mowanjum, for its part, has built a 3D Wandjina cave for education.[^T-079]

The Historian's file draws a line that I will mark as interpretation. The alien reading continues an older pattern that starts with Grey: doubt that Aboriginal people made, or understood, their own art. The live issue for the custodians is cultural property and the right to say what the images mean. **(author analysis)**

That changes how the opening question applies. "They had no word for what they saw" assumes the makers were describing something outside their world. The Wandjina's custodians say the opposite: they know exactly what the figures are, and they have names for them. A reader can still ask the question. But the people with the most standing to answer it already have. **(author analysis)**

## Barrier Canyon Style: the Great Gallery, Horseshoe Canyon, Utah

**What the record says.** In Horseshoe Canyon, a detached unit of Canyonlands National Park in Utah, a long panel called the Great Gallery holds life-size painted figures in what archaeologists call the Barrier Canyon Style. The tallest group is nicknamed the "Holy Ghost." The figures are tapering, armless or nearly so, with large eyes and no clear legs.[^T-080]

Their age was disputed for decades. Earlier estimates placed the style in the Early or Late Archaic, and one NEH article cites a hair date of 6750 BCE for Barrier Canyon Style generally. In 2014, Joel Pederson and colleagues published a study in *PNAS* using luminescence dating and the order in which features overlap. They placed the Great Gallery panel between about A.D. 1 and 1100, ruling out earlier hypotheses of 7,000 to 8,000 years.[^T-080]

Archaeologists read the figures as spirit, shamanic, or guardian beings. Polly Schaafsma's reading is summarized by the Natural History Museum of Utah, and Steven Sucec calls them "spirit figures."[^T-080]

**The custodians.** The National Park Service funded an ethnographic study by Richard Stoffle and colleagues, based on 316 interviews at Canyonlands with Hopi, Zuni, Northern and Southern Ute, Paiute, and Navajo representatives. Navajo representatives said that Barrier Canyon Style images at Bartlett Alcove were made by Creator Beings who passed into another dimension there.[^T-080]

That testimony needs to be read carefully. "Creator Beings who passed into another dimension" can sound, to a reader steeped in UFO literature, like a description of visitors. But it is a living religious belief stated in its own terms. It is not a ufological claim, and this chapter does not treat it as one. **(author analysis)**

**Who first drew the spacemen parallel.** This is **unconfirmed**. Online sources note only the figures' "alien-like" appearance. The Historian found no first printed ancient-astronaut work about the Great Gallery.[^T-080] **Open question (OPEN_QUESTIONS #31):** the first printed ancient-astronaut or "spacemen" reading of the Great Gallery.

Nor did the Historian find any published tribal objection aimed specifically at an alien reading of these figures.[^T-080] That is also listed as open. It would be easy to borrow the Wandjina objection and apply it here, and that would be wrong. Each community speaks for itself.

**How the image travels.** A 1940 WPA mural copy of the panel hangs in the Natural History Museum of Utah. The site is a popular hike, and the "Holy Ghost" nickname feeds otherworldly readings.[^T-080]

The 2014 dating also changes the story people tell about the panel. A figure believed to be 8,000 years old invites romance about lost ages. A figure painted within the last two thousand years belongs to a much better known world of farming and foraging peoples on the Colorado Plateau. **(author analysis)**

## What the four sites have in common

Set side by side, the four sites follow a pattern, though not a uniform one. **(author analysis)**

At every site, the archaeological record offers a reading in the makers' own world: warriors in crested helmets at Zurla, masked and costumed people at Tassili, rain-bringing spirit ancestors in the Kimberley, and spirit or guardian beings in Horseshoe Canyon.[^T-077][^T-078][^T-079][^T-080]

Where living custodians exist and have spoken in the sources found, they describe creator beings in their own tradition: the Wandjina for the Mowanjum peoples, and the Creator Beings of Navajo testimony at Canyonlands.[^T-079][^T-080] Only at the Wandjina did the sources include a direct objection to the alien reading.[^T-079]

The spacemen readings all come after 1947. The earliest one we can date with confidence is Lhote's, in 1958, and it reads as a joke.[^T-078] The Wandjina reading dates to von Däniken in 1968, so far as anyone has found.[^T-079] For Val Camonica and the Great Gallery, the first author is still unknown.[^T-077][^T-080]

Two things recur in how the reading is made. Each time, the reading works by picking out a feature and comparing it to something modern: rays become antennae, a round head becomes a helmet, a halo becomes a space suit. And the figure is often removed from its setting: cropped out of its duel, separated from its custodians, or given an older date than the evidence supports. **(author analysis)**

## Closing: the question stays open

Return to the disc on the water. Someone who has no word for it calls it a pot, because a pot is what they know.

The four sites in this chapter let us ask that question from both directions. Did the people who carved and painted these figures see something they had no words for, and draw it with the shapes they knew? Or did modern readers, after 1947, see shapes they had no words for, and reach for the nearest thing they knew, which was a spaceman? The makers' world gave them warriors, masks, rain, and creators. Ours gave us helmets and antennae.

I do not answer that question for you. The record above is what we have: dates, descriptions, the words of custodians where they have spoken, and the names and years of the people who first said "spacemen," where we know them. Where we don't, the gaps are marked.

Chapter 9 picks up the same habit at a larger scale. It follows modern writers from Charles Fort to Erich von Däniken as they read old texts and images as records of visitation, and it asks the same two questions about each one.
